\reftext{The use of speech as an interaction technique in virtual reality (VR) applications is becoming increasingly popular [1-3]. One obvious reason is the vast improvement in the quality of processing of spoken and written natural language [4,5]. This is particularly evident in the significant public attention drawn by chatbots like ChatGPT. Advances in natural language processing (NLP) have also made speech technologies interesting as a means of locomotion for VR applications, especially in scenarios where the hands are unavailable because they are used for secondary tasks [2,6,7]. These scenarios, also described as hands-free, occur for example, in the medical field in surgical procedures under sterile conditions [8,9]. Other fields, such as architecture and art, utilize voice commands to quickly navigate and manipulate scenes [10], thereby increasing the accessibility of applications and enabling users to participate in virtual exhibitions and museums that they could not physically visit. Voice navigation also provides an alternative means of interaction for individuals for whom the use of physical controls is impractical or infeasible [11,12].}

\reftext{The effectiveness of using voice control for hands-free navigation in environments with distinct visual objects has been well-documented in several studies [2,3,6]. However, many VR applications face challenges when there are no prominent visual objects, commonly referred to as landmarks, or when describing these landmarks in natural language is not feasible. The goal of our work is to address this gap by providing a voice-based navigation solution for environments that lack sufficient landmarks. This issue arises in both fundamental environments, such as empty houses or rooms, as well as in specialized environments like those used for visualizing biomedical models [13,14]. In these contexts, the absence of visual landmarks can make it difficult to articulate precise positions, both for navigating oneself and for placing virtual objects or markers in specific locations. A similar challenge arises when an object cannot be clearly articulated, such as in a forest, where identifying the exact tree the user intends to navigate to becomes a cumbersome task. In the absence of a robust speech interface and hands-free locomotion technique, this process can become an errorprone and frustrating dialog between the user and the system, leading to a less satisfactory experience. We directly address the challenges of landmark-based voice navigation as a critical step towards enabling truly immersive and versatile VR experiences.}

\reftext{To close this gap in voice-based locomotion, we explore the concept of user-centered coordinate systems as a potential solution. Aeronautics [15] serves as a reference field due to its well-established and effective navigation systems. In aeronautics, sophisticated coordinate systems, such as Cartesian and spherical systems, have been employed to tackle the challenges of navigating complex and dynamic environments from a user-centered perspective. By utilizing user-centered coordinate systems to pinpoint locations in both 2D and 3D, we enable a form of interaction with digital environments that was previously not possible in voice-based locomotion. Hence, we undertake a comparison of user-centered coordinate systems through four distinct tasks: (i) 2D navigation in environments with constant height, (ii) 3D navigation in spatial environments with different heights, (iii) 2D object manipulation in environments with constant height, and (iv) 3D object manipulation in spatial environments with different heights. Our goal is to explore the practicality of these tasks and identify usage patterns that could further improve hands-free navigation. Our contributions can be summarized as follows:}

\begin{itemize}
\item \reftext{Development of a real-time voice control system for speechbased locomotion and object placement in environments lacking visual landmarks.}
\item \reftext{Comparison of user-centered Cartesian and spherical coordinate systems for hands-free locomotion in VR .}
\item \reftext{Identifying and analyzing usage patterns for speech-based locomotion and object placement tasks.}
\item \reftext{Offering a open-source access to encourage further research and development in the field of voice-based navigation.1}
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

In this section, we look at previous work. In Section 2.1, we focus on scenario specification languages and temporal logic, which define what a scenario and its tasks are. In Section 2.2, we focus on the generation of urban road scenarios, particularly LLM-based techniques. In Section 2.3, we refer to related work in Human-Computer Interaction that focuses on human-in-the-loop experiences as well as pedestrian experiences in VR, as our generation system aims to create these experiences.

\subsection{Scenario Specifications and Temporal Logic}
\subsubsection{Scenario Specification Languages and their Standards}
Scenario specification has been classified in 4 levels of details as shown in figure~\ref{fig:scenario_spec_levels}. ASAM OpenScenario~\cite{asam_openscenario} and ASAM OpenDrive~\cite{todo} are exchange formats for dynamic content and road networks. ASAM OpenScenario is a DSL aimed which is often considered as a standard to define dynamic content of an urban road scenarios. It has later been updated~\cite{todo cite openscenario 2} to support logical as well as abstract features. ESMINI~\cite{todo cite esmini} is a frontend which can render OpenScenario scenarios in a 3D environment. Similarly, several other declarative and probabilistic scenario languages have been released~\cite{fremont2019scenic,vin2023scenic3,4JPQ8JTG,J3YJDXUH,VYKWQ4VL,YBT8TZBC,JMP3L7FI,SPU4TMNS,CB7JIPN2,todo cite geoscenario} to create urban road scenarios. Note that formats like OpenScenario are mostly declaritive and require constraints to simulate real-life road behaviour while probabilistic languages like Scenic are more imperative for road behaviour and thus allow to define distributions over the parameters of the scenario~\cite{molin2023specguided}. However, none of these techniques allow the pedestrian to be the ego/main actor of the scenario and perform guided tasks. 

\subsubsection{Temporal Logic and Robustness}
Maler and Nickovic~\cite{maler2004monitoring} introduced Signal Temporal Logic (STL), a framework to specify and monitor properties of continuous-time, real-valued signals using time-bounded operators such as \emph{always}, \emph{eventually} and \emph{until}. Fainekos and Pappas~\cite{fainekos2009robustness} replaced the true/false answer with a degree of satisfaction, i.e.\ how far a signal is from violating a specification, and Donz\'e et al.~\cite{donze2010robust} extended this to a robust semantics for real-valued signals that measures robustness in both value and time. Corso et al.~\cite{corso2021blackbox} survey such black-box safety validation algorithms and and Ramezani et al.~\cite{ramezani2019semantics} show on an autonomous driving example that the choice of robustness semantics affects falsification performance. In these works, robustness is used to test a controller or a vehicle. We use the same quantitative semantics differently: the signed margin of a task specification is evaluated on the real pose of a human pedestrian, both when validating a generated scenario and live during play.

\subsection{Scenario Generation}
\subsubsection{LLM-based Temporal Logic and Scenario Generation}
Recently, several works have used LLMs to generate temporal logic specifications from natural language prompts. nl2spec~\cite{cosler2023nl2spec} and NL2TL~\cite{chen2023nl2tl} translate natural language to temporal logic, and Lang2LTL~\cite{liu2023lang2ltl} grounds natural-language commands to temporal tasks in unseen environments for robots. Even though these works provide a way to produce temporal logic specifications from natural language, they stop at the formula: to our knowledge, none of them checks the specification against a real map or turns it into an executable scenario. OmniTester~\cite{todo} proposes a method to create AD testing scenarios using Multi LLMs by creating OpenStreetMap road geometry described via a prompt and then retrieving using embeddings. 

Apart from temporal logic specifications, several works have used LLMs to generate urban road scenarios as well. Text2Scenario~\cite{todo cite text2scenario} generates OpenScenario code and runs it on CARLA~\cite{todo cite carla} based on a given user prompt to enable quicker Autonomous Driving testing. Similarly, Txt2Sce~\cite{txt2sce2025} generates OpenScenario code from textual accident reports. Muller et al.~\cite{muller2025qcopendrive} uses LLMs with the QC-OpenDRIVE framework in order to generate syntactically and semantically correct OpenDRIVE road networks. 

Several methods have also used LLMs to generate intermediate representations to allow validation against a real map. Safa et al.~\cite{safa2026extendedscenic} presents an LLM pipeline with an Extended Scenic DSL between free text and simulation scripts. TARGET~\cite{target2023} generates an intermediate DSL based on given traffic rules to allow  syntactic and semantic validation. Miceli et al.~\cite{miceli2023dialogue} uses dialog as input to generate Scenic code and renders the scenarios using CARLA with a validation feedback.

Another line of work creates safety-critical urban scenarios for testing a vehicle, where other road users, including pedestrians, act as adversaries. ChatScene~\cite{zhang2024chatscene} uses an LLM agent with few-shot examples and a retrieval process to assemble Scenic code that runs on the CARLA simulator. ScenicNL~\cite{elmaaroufi2024scenicnl} generates Scenic code from unstructured prompts using an LLM and a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipeline. TTSG~\cite{ruan2024ttsg} generates traffic scenes from prompts through road retrieval and an LLM-based agent planning pipeline. ChatDyn~\cite{chatdyn2024} uses LLMs to generate multi-actor dynamics in street scenes. AGENTS-LLM~\cite{yao2025agentsllm} presents a scenario augmentation framework that takes an existing scenario and a user prompt as input and modifies the scenario using LLM agents in different roles, such as modifier, quality assurance and visual agents.

To our knowledge~\cite{zhao2025llmsurvey, zhou2025genaisurvey} all these works target specifically AD testing and do not allow the human pedestrian to act as the ego/main actor of the scene performing a set of tasks in the described scenario. 


\subsubsection{Urban and 3D scene world generation}
MetaUrban~\cite{wu2025metaurban} provides an embodied RL simulation platform for urban micromobility. UrbanWorld~\cite{shang2024urbanworld} uses LLMs to plan a scene using a prompt and then use blender along with diffusion models to procedurally generate 3D scenes from layouts. CityX~\cite{zhang2024cityx} uses LLM agent for planning urban areas and geneerate them procedurally using Blender and Procedural Content Generation plugins. LayoutGPT~\cite{feng2023layoutgpt} uses LLM with a CSS like layout structure to plan and place 3D assets to create various 2D and 3D scenes.

\reftext{This section offers an overview of the various methods compared in this study. In Section 3.1, we investigate the distinct properties of Cartesian, cylindrical, and spherical coordinate systems. Section 3.2 describes the NLP architecture employed for recognizing speech commands.}

\subsection{User-centered coordinate systems}
\reftext{We are comparing three coordinate systems for voice-controlled locomotion: Cartesian, cylindrical, and spherical. All systems are inspired by concepts from the field of aeronautics [15], but their design, interaction methods, and use cases are tailored to our specific approach. In our study, these systems are applied to movements in flat environments, changes in spatial positions, and the placement of objects. The coordinate system was always aligned with the user's view direction, meaning that if the user turned to the left, the system would immediately follow and remain centered within the user's field of view. Several core principles were considered in the design of these locomotion systems to ensure usability and precision. Each system was developed based on distinct guiding principles. The cylindrical system, however, represents a hybrid approach that combines elements from both the Cartesian and spherical navigation paradigms.}

\reftext{Usability and simplicity. The cartesian coordinate system aims to provide a user-friendly interaction with minimal cognitive load. To achieve this, we implemented a single-number encoding, enabling users to specify a position using a single number. This simplicity is crucial for minimizing user errors and improving the overall experience. This approach is supported by Sweller's cognitive load theory [73] and Mayer's principles for reducing cognitive load [74].}

\reftext{Precision and flexibility. The spherical coordinate system employs an encoding method specifically designed to enhance precision. This system utilizes longitude, latitude, and distance values to achieve floatingpoint accuracy. It was specifically developed for scenarios that require more accurate navigation, striking a balance between usability and functionality. Research by Mine et al. [75] and LaViola et al. [76] highlights that precise selection techniques enhance user interaction by minimizing errors and speeding up task completion.}

\reftext{User interface. As voice input is the primary mode of interaction with our system, the user interface is intentionally minimalist. When the user presses the trigger on the left-hand controller, the microphone is activated and begins recording. Upon release, the captured voice input is processed. To provide clear feedback, a small sphere is attached to the digital representation of the user's left hand. This sphere appears green while the microphone is active and red when it is inactive, ensuring that the user is aware of the microphone's status and increasing confidence in the system's responsiveness. Additionally, the recognized voice is displayed as text on the digital left hand. This feature helps to reduce user frustration in cases where speech input may be misunderstood by making the system's transcription of the command visible. The annotations are always attached to the digital left hand and move in real time with the user's hand, ensuring that feedback remains visible and contextually relevant during interaction.}

\subsubsection{Cartesian coordinate systems}
\reftext{In Fig. 1, we present an example of our Cartesian coordinate system design. This design features rectangular grids that are generated in front of the user's position, synchronously updating as they move. Grid elements can be labeled with: (a) numbers, (b) characters, or (c) hybrid combinations of both, akin to the labeling of chessboards. In our case, we choose to use only numerical values. The grid coordinates range from -999 to 999, with the z-x plane spanning from 0 to 99. The yaxis represents height, with values in the hundreds of digits. This setup enables users to intuitively encode changes in their position, associating two-digit numbers, such as 12, with shifts in the z-x plane. Using the hundreds digit, like 123, elevates the user by one unit in the y-direction and positions them in grid 23 of the z-x plane. The distinction between two-digit and three-digit numbers was intentional, as locomotion is often only two-dimensional. In such cases, specifying three digits, where the first digit would always be zero, is unnecessary. To reduce effort, users can simply articulate ``23'' instead of ``023'' when only 2D locomotion is required. Negative numbers, e.g., -156, move the user down one unit in the y-direction and to the 2D horizontal grid 56. Due to the decision to use negative numbers to represent height, we are unable to assign negative values for left and right movement, as this approach is designed to encode locomotion using a single numerical value. Consequently, the user's central reference positions are defined as 004 and 005. The grids are sorted in local yzx order relative to the user, facilitating quick location changes by memory. For instance, the grids 004 and 005 represent the units directly in front of the user. This logical arrangement ensures that the grids align with the user as the origin of the local coordinate system for navigation. While the step size between grid points can be customized, we have selected a default distance of 1 meter. This setting can be adjusted in real time to create finer or larger steps, thereby modifying the overall dimensions of the grid. However, for the study, we chose to limit the default distance between grid points to 1 meter to align the distances used in the cylindrical and spherical approaches, ensuring the comparability of results. The color and opacity of the grids are customizable based on the background. As the default, we utilize a light blue color with an opacity of 0.5. This choice ensures that the grids stand out conspicuously without completely obscuring the surrounding environment.}

\reftext{Implementation. Within the Cartesian system, each location is represented as a single plane featuring a slightly transparent texture overlaid with a text field. When the system is activated, 100 such tiles are initialized and arranged in a compact grid in front of the user. These tiles are then numbered according to our encoding scheme. Once a voice command is recognized and a target number is identified, the system locates the corresponding tile and selects its center as the user's new destination. The Y-Value Tower is implemented in a similar manner but differs in spatial configuration. Here, the planes are arranged vertically in a straight upward formation, with each plane oriented to face the user.}

\subsubsection{Spherical coordinate systems}
\reftext{In Fig. 3(b), we present an illustration of our spherical coordinate approach. The spheres are centered around the user, and their positions are defined by a longitude (lon) angle ranging from -180 to 180 , a latitude (lat) angle between -90 and 90 , and a radius. This design enables users to pinpoint a specific point in 3D space by expressing the spherical coordinates as 2D lon-lat coordinates, with the distance represented by the radius. If only a single angle is specified without additional markers, it defaults to the lon, facilitating 2D spherical navigation. In cases where no radius is specified, it defaults to a radius of one. When used solely with distance and longitude values as a 2D locomotion technique, the spherical system effectively represents the use of a polar coordinate system. The visual representation of the 2D spherical system consists of multiple circles based on different radii, with a default distance of 1 m between each circle. Longitude angles are labeled in 10 increments at a 5 m distance. Upon entering 3D spherical navigation, an additional circle appears at the 0 longitude position, oriented along the latitude direction. Here, extra labels are added at 10 intervals. While the labels are placed at 10 intervals, users enjoy the flexibility to freely select any value within the range of the minimum and maximum possible angles as their input. Similar to the grid, sphere coloring adheres to the principles described earlier. Users can adjust the degree intervals in both the lon and lat directions of the sphere according to their preferences.}

\reftext{Y-axis, ensuring that it remains horizontally aligned and consistently flat on the ground.}

\subsubsection{Cylindrical coordinate systems}
\reftext{In Fig. 2(a), we present an illustration of our cylindrical coordinate approach. This system represents a hybrid between Cartesian and spherical coordinate systems. This fusion allows for intuitive navigation and positioning in a cylindrical reference frame. The system is centered around the user and defines positions using a longitude (lon) angle ranging from -180 to 180 , a forward distance extending radially from the user, and a height component that extends vertically, either upward or downward along the y-axis. In this representation, spatial locations in 3D are encoded using a combination of angular direction and a compact numerical encoding of distance and height. For instance, a label such as ``35'' denotes a point located 3 meters upward and 5 meters forward in the direction specified by the longitude angle. This notation enables an intuitive and compact way to specify positions within the cylindrical frame, while still providing accurate positioning options. The visual layout of the 2D cylindrical system consists of concentric radial lines centering around the user, with angular intervals labeled every 10 and distance markings placed in one meter increments. In the 3D cylindrical mode, a tower indicating the height at one meter intervals.}

\reftext{Implementation. The cylindrical system is implemented in a manner similar to the spherical system but differs in its representation of height information. Instead of rendering a spherical slice, it employs a linear vertical height encoding. Once a voice command is processed, the system first identifies the corresponding position on the ground plane and then elevates the user vertically based on the tens digit of the input number.}

\subsubsection{Teleportation}
\reftext{To establish a baseline for comparison with the voice-based approaches, we included on of the most commonly used navigation method in VR, teleportation, into our evaluation [18]. It is important to note that, unlike the voice-based systems, teleportation still relies on physical controllers and cannot be considered a hands-free approach. It is primarily used as a comparison point in this work. In the teleportation system, users initiate the teleportation by pressing the touchpad on the controller. While the button is held down, a parabolic line is projected from the controller, showing a preview of the teleport destination. This line intersects with the environment, turning green when aimed at a valid location and red when directed at an invalid area. Upon releasing the button, teleportation is initiated and the screen fades to black. The user is then teleported to the point where the line intersects with the environment, after which the screen fades back in. The same locomotion method is also employed for all voice-based approaches. Once the desired destination is determined, the screen fades to black, teleportation is performed, and the screen fades back in. This approach is intended to prevent sudden movements that may induce motion sickness. The implementation is based on the default teleportation functionality provided by SteamVR.}

\subsection{Voice control system}
\reftext{This section examines the voice control system, detailing its architecture and the commands it supports. Unlike traditional systems that rely on specific keywords, our system uses semantic mapping to interpret user intentions, allowing various phrasings.}

\subsubsection{System architecture}
\reftext{Our voice control system comprises two main components: a speechto-text module and a language model module. Both are implemented as services within a FastAPI server, which runs in the background inside a Docker container. The Unity front-end application [77] communicates with these services via web requests. To capture user speech, we continuously record audio using the Valve Index microphone at a sampling rate of 16 kHz. The raw audio stream is directed to the speech-to-text service on the server. To delineate the start and end of a voice command, users could toggle the microphone using a button to mute or unmute it. The recorded WAV audio file is then processed by a Whisper [5] speech-to-text transcription model. We utilize the small version of Whisper with 8-bit quantization to ensure real-time transcription performance. Once transcription is complete, the resulting text string is forwarded to the language model service. Within this service, a conversation manager processes the dialog. It constructs a prompt that incorporates a set of callable functions, either predefined or dynamically added at runtime. Each callable function must include a docstring describing its signature, parameters, and purpose. The transcribed user query is embedded into this prompt and sent to a Gemini 2.0 Flash language model instance via Google Cloud Vertex AI for text completion. Based on the input query, the language model determines the appropriate function to invoke and generates a response in the form of a function call with the correct arguments, enabling seamless execution of voice-driven commands. This string is then sent back to the Unity front-end, where the function is finally executed. Our code and a detailed architecture description are available as an open-source repository at <anonymized link>.}

\subsubsection{Voice command types}
\reftext{Our voice control system implements three different types of locomotion commands: (i) absolute positioning commands, (ii) relative positioning commands, and (iii) specific commands.}

\reftext{Absolute positioning commands. Absolute positioning commands allow you to specify a desired position in the Cartesian, cylindrical, and spherical coordinate system. To change the position on the Cartesian grid, the user simply specifies the desired position by the grid number, such as `Go to grid 544'. After the speech-to-text transcription, the language model identifies the correct function and its parameter and initiates the execution of the function call move\_player\_grid(544) in the Unity engine. After execution, the user's position will be adjusted to the 544th grid. Similarly with the cylindrical and spherical coordinate system. In the spherical approach the user specifies a location change in longitude, latitude, and radius as follows `Move to 30 degrees longitude, 15 degrees latitude, and 5 meters'. The transcribed audio is interpreted by the language model and the correct function is called with the parameter list 30, 15, 5 ending in the function call move\_player\_sphere(30,15,5).}

\reftext{Relative positioning commands. Relative positioning commands offer a flexible way to specify changes in position or orientation based on the user's current context and are applicable across all navigation techniques, regardless of the underlying coordinate system. These commands can be used for self-locomotion tasks, modifying the user's own position or orientation, as well as for object placement tasks, where an object's position or rotation is adjusted relative to its current state. For example, a command such as ``Move one step to the right '' can be interpreted as move\_player\_relative(`Right', 1) and moves the player relatively to its own position. Similarly, orientation adjustments, such as ``Turn the sofa 45 degrees'', are interpreted as rotate\_object(`Sofa', 45), with rotation applied around the Y-axis by default. This approach enables users to adjust both their own position and the position of virtual objects in the environment more effectively and intuitively.}

\reftext{Special commands. Special commands are mainly used to trigger additional functions, such as building visual aids like grid towers (`Show me a tower at 85' calls build\_grid\_tower(85)) for better estimation of height differences in Cartesian coordinate systems, and activating latitude slices in spheres for better estimation of elevation angles (`Show me sphere slice at 20 degrees' calls show\_sphere\_slice(20)). A visual representation of this is shown in Figs. 1, 2 and in the lower row of Fig. 5.}

\subsection{Study setup}
\reftext{To ensure the comparability of study results, we conducted the experiment in a controlled, in-person, in-lab environment. Before starting the study, we conducted a trial to ensure that all participants understood the tasks and commands. This training phase lasted a maximum of five minutes, during which we addressed any questions. During the study, only the participant and the research staff were present to minimize external influences. To further mitigate unintended voice commands, participants could mute and unmute the microphone by pressing a button on the left-hand controller. Upon completing the study, each participant was asked to complete a questionnaire and participate in a brief debriefing session. On average, the entire process took approximately 60 min per participant, depending on individual speed and engagement. The study was developed using the Unity framework [77]. We employed a system consisting of an Intel(R) Core i9-12900K processor, an Nvidia RTX 3090 graphics card, 32 GB of RAM, and Windows 11. This configuration allowed us to run our VR setup and voice control system without encountering any performance issues. For the HMD, we opted for the Valve Index.}

\subsection{Procedure}
\reftext{The study consisted of four main tasks: (1) 2D navigation, (2) 3D navigation, (3) 2D object manipulation, and (4) 3D object manipulation. Each task was performed a total of four times, once for each of the locomotion techniques: the Cartesian, cylindrical, and spherical voice-based approaches, as well as teleportation, which served as the non-voice-based baseline technique. 2D navigation. We utilized a simplified maze to evaluate the navigation capabilities of voice-controlled locomotion. Participants were tasked with navigating a maze (see Fig. 4(a)) consisting of a predefined path that involved left and right turns. There was no wrong turn within the maze. The maze included four barriers that partially blocked the user's view to the next turn, adding visual clutter to better mimic real-world navigation challenges. The barriers were placed on the left, right, top, and bottom sides of the maze. The maze was designed so that each turn could still be reached with a single voice command, regardless of the navigation system used. Thus, to complete the maze optimally, at least seven voice commands were required: six for the turns and one to reach the goal. 3D navigation. One limitation of previous voice locomotion techniques is the inability to navigate across different height levels. Therefore, we constructed a three-dimensional environment with multiple platforms suspended in the clouds (see Fig. 4(b)). Each of the seven platforms was uniquely positioned at different locations with different heights. As a result, participants had to navigate upwards and downwards three times each throughout the experiment. The distance between each platform was designed to be reachable with a single voice command, regardless of the navigation system employed. Completing this task optimally required at least six voice commands (seven platforms, excluding the starting platform).}

\reftext{Object manipulation. For the task of object manipulation, each participant was presented with a miniature model of a room containing six distinct furniture items, along with a full-sized, empty room (see Fig. 6). The goal was to accurately and efficiently recreate the modeled environment. Certain objects, such as the bed, required only correct placement within the scene. Others, including the sofa and desk, also needed to be rotated using voice commands. Additionally, items like the lamp, vase, and mirror featured distinct height levels, introducing further complexity to their placement. To complete the task effectively, participants required a minimum of eight voice commands: six for placing each object and two for the required rotations.}

\reftext{Study order and counterbalancing. To ensure a progressively increasing level of difficulty, the study tasks were carefully ordered, beginning with the simplest and advancing to more complex ones. The sequence participants were informed about the purpose of the study and provided informed consent prior to taking part. Educational backgrounds among participants varied, with 8 having completed a master's program, 14 holding a bachelor's degree, and 2 possessing a Ph.D. Regarding VR usage, 8 participants had never used VR before, 6 had used it once or twice, 9 reported using it a few times per year, and 1 participant used VR more than a few times per month. All participants had a background in information technology, either as software engineers or currently engaged in research or as students within a related field.}

\subsection{Measures}
\reftext{The following section outlines the types of measures employed in our study, which are divided into objective and subjective categories. Subjective measures were collected through a post-study questionnaire administered via a Google Forms document. These measures capture participants' self-reported experiences, preferences, and perceptions related to the interaction techniques. Objective measures were recorded in real time within the Unity environment during task execution. Each objective metric was computed or automatically captured at runtime, enabling precise and consistent data collection without user intervention.}

\reftext{In our study, the independent variables include the interaction modality (voice-based vs. teleportation), the coordinate system used for locomotion (Cartesian, cylindrical, and spherical), and the task type (2D navigation, 3D navigation, 2D object manipulation, and 3D object manipulation). The dependent variables comprise both objective and subjective measures. Objective metrics include task completion time, number of voice commands issued, and spatial accuracy. Subjective metrics were obtained through standardized questionnaires and assess sickness, comfort, presence, and usability.}

\subsubsection{Objective measurements}
\reftext{In our evaluation, we have incorporated three objective measurements. The first commonly used metric in evaluating locomotion systems is the time to target [3,16,18], which measures the time participants require to complete a given task. Additionally, we are interested in the number of voice commands needed to accomplish the task. To gain a better understanding of the participants' interaction pattern, we have recorded the corresponding target locations including grid numbers or longitude/latitude values. For tasks involving object manipulation, accuracy is an important factor [40,41,81]. Therefore, we recorded the Euclidean distance error in units of measurement. A small distance from the gold standard results in a high accuracy.}

\subsubsection{Subjective measurements}
\reftext{The questionnaires chosen as subjective measures are generally validated and widely used. However, in some cases, they may not fully align with our focus on speech-based locomotion. We adapted the questionnaires to better suit our research needs. For example, the ``Device Assessment Questionnaire (DAQ)'' includes questions on physical fatigue related to finger, wrist, arm, and shoulder movements, which are irrelevant in our hands-free navigation context and could lead to misleading results. This streamlined approach ensures that we focus on questions most relevant to assessing locomotion tasks, in line with previous studies [3,18] and recommendations [82]. We have documented the selection process for each questionnaire to provide clarity and context for our findings, striking a balance between our study's unique requirements and the need to align with existing literature. All questions are answered using either a 5-point or 7-point Likert scale, depending on the recommended format specified by each questionnaire.}

\reftext{Sickness. One of the most crucial criteria for user-centered applications is to ensure that users are not experiencing any sickness within the environment. Regardless of how accurate and fast an application may be, if it causes discomfort, it is unlikely to find practical application. Therefore, we have incorporated questions 1, 3, 4, and 8 from the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ) [83]. These questions specifically address aspects such as general discomfort, headache, eye strain, and nausea.}

\reftext{Comfort. While a high sickness score may serve as an indicator against adopting the system, ensuring user comfort is essential for evaluating the convenience of a system. A higher comfort score indicates a greater likelihood that users will embrace the system. We have adapted the Device Assessment Questionnaire (DAQ) [84], incorporating questions 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6. These questions encompass various aspects such as overall comfort, ease of use, accuracy, and physical and mental exertion.}

\reftext{Presence. In the context of virtual environments, achieving a sense of presence or immersion is essential for fully engaging users in the digital world. To evaluate the level of immersion across different locomotion systems, we use the Igroup Presence Questionnaire (IPQ) [85]. We selected all questions from the IPQ, which cover various aspects of presence that can be categorized into general presence, spatial presence, involvement, and experienced realism. The work of Melo et al. [86] investigated the thresholds at which presence scores can be considered acceptable. We utilize their grading framework to contextualize our results in relation to a broad range of applications that employ the IPQ for evaluating presence. Melo et al. categorized presence scores into letter grades ranging from A to F, A being the highest.}

\reftext{Usability. The usability of the system is important for an intuitive user experience. To assess this, we employed the System Usability Scale (SUS) [87], which consists of ten questions. These questions cover various aspects of usability and have been categorized into the following: reusability, complexity, integration, consistency, learnability, and confidence.}

\reftext{The following section addresses Research Questions 1-4 (see Section 4.1), discusses potential directions for future work, and outlines the inherent limitations of our system. Research Questions 1-2 focus exclusively on evaluating the performance of our voice-based approaches, while Research Question 3-5 compares their performance to the non-voice-based baseline of teleportation.}

\subsection{RQ1: Locomotion tasks}
\reftext{In our study, we investigated two different approaches to handsfree voice interaction within a digital environment: self-locomotion of the user and the locomotion or manipulation of external objects. This distinction was made to assess whether these systems have different application areas. It is important to treat these tasks separately and avoid comparing subjective measures between them.}

\reftext{However, our objective measures indicate that, within the same coordinate system, self-locomotion and object manipulation tasks produced very similar scores across most categories. Despite the similarity, self-locomotion consistently received slightly higher scores in every category. We also found that none of the coordinate systems or tasks induced any form of sickness during use. Furthermore, all systems received high presence scores, with no notable differences between them. Applying the framework of Melo et al. [86] to our presence results, all techniques lie within the acceptable range and achieved a grade of B for experienced realism, B for spatial presence, A for involvement, and A for general presence. Thus, both systems passed critical assessments and can be incorporated into any VR application without posing health risks or compromising immersion.}

\reftext{When comparing our results to similar voice-based work by Hombeck et al. [3,72], we found that most metrics, including sickness and presence scores, align closely with those observed in our system. However, slight differences emerged in terms of mental effort and complexity. These factors were slightly worse in our work compared to voice-based navigation systems that include landmarks. However, when compared to voice user interfaces for data interaction, our work showed better results. It is important to note that the work of Hombeck et al. [3] only included a 2D navigation task. The reduced mental effort observed in their results may stem from the simpler phrasing required to reach the destination, but it could also be influenced by the lower task complexity. In comparison to voice-based interaction with data, our system demonstrated higher user involvement scores, with participants reporting a stronger sense of immersion and realism. While the accuracy for other voice-based systems were similar, the speed of usage in our system showed an improvement, likely due to advancements in the voice system itself.}

\reftext{During the feedback sessions, we found that most participants perceived moving themselves as slightly more convenient than moving objects. However, they acknowledged the benefits of both systems, especially in scenarios where an alternative method of interaction is required. Thus, the Cartesian, cylindrical, and spherical locomotion techniques did not exhibit notable differences when applied to either self-locomotion or object manipulation, indicating their suitability for both tasks. However, significant differences were observed between the Cartesian, cylindrical, and spherical approaches themselves.}

\subsection{RQ2: Coordinate system}
\reftext{To evaluate the comparison between the Cartesian, cylindrical, and spherical coordinate systems, we employed multiple subjective and objective measures. Across self-locomotion as well as object manipulation approaches, the Cartesian system was consistently found to be more accurate, faster, and more user-friendly. While all systems performed similarly in object manipulation tasks, a clear preference emerged during self-locomotion tasks. Out of the 24 participants, 16 favored the Cartesian system, appreciating its simplicity, particularly the use of a single number for referencing positions instead of a combination of angles and distances. Eight participants preferred the cylindrical system, while none favored the spherical approach. This preference was further reflected in usability questionnaire responses, where the Cartesian system scored higher in categories such as reusability, complexity, learnability, and confidence. Similarly, it outperformed the cylindrical and spherical systems in terms of ease of use, mental effort, and accuracy. These results were largely influenced by the simpler positional encoding used in the Cartesian system, a point emphasized in feedback from nearly all participants. During the study, we also observed that some participants became confused about the distinction between longitude and latitude angles when using the spherical approach. This occasionally led to unintended, though correctly interpreted, locomotion commands. Although this issue was limited to a few participants, it may help explain the higher reported mental effort associated with the spherical coordinate system. Furthermore, in the Cartesian system, the encoding was continuously displayed on the ground, whereas in the cylindrical and spherical systems, only guiding lines were visible and the exact position had to be estimated. This difference may have influenced the perceived mental effort. However, the error in the object manipulation task was similar across all voice-based conditions, suggesting that even when participants had to estimate the target position, the resulting accuracy was comparable to conditions in which numerical values were continuously displayed.}

\reftext{While we observe differences in mental effort between the techniques, it is important to note that, in the current setup, participants had their hands free and were not engaged in additional tasks, such as moving objects or performing intricate fine motor activities. Performing secondary tasks would likely further increase the mental complexity, making it even more critical to minimize the cognitive load associated with locomotion. Although the precise impact of secondary tasks on mental effort requires further investigation, it should be carefully considered when selecting an appropriate locomotion technique, further highlighting the advantages of the Cartesian encoding.}

\reftext{While the number of commands used to complete a task was quite similar, notable differences in task completion time emerged. Although the spherical system was designed for greater precision, its increased complexity ultimately resulted in similar or even slightly worse accuracy, along with longer completion times in practice, compared to the more intuitive Cartesian approach. This can again be attributed to the more complex position encoding required by the spherical system. The goal of creating a system that combined elements of both the Cartesian and spherical approaches resulted in a system whose performance consistently fell between the two. The cylindrical system generally performed better than the spherical approach across most tasks but remained less effective than the Cartesian system. Although it was favored by some participants and consistently outperformed the spherical approach, it was never able to surpass the Cartesian system in overall performance.}

\reftext{Therefore, we would strongly advocate for the Cartesian encoding method. This approach offers faster completion times while maintaining the same level of accuracy and the same number of required voice commands.}

\subsection{RQ3: Interaction modality}
\reftext{To compare the concept of voice-based locomotion against a baseline VR locomotion technique, we integrated teleportation into our study. While the teleportation approach demonstrated higher performance across most categories, voice-based systems proved capable of approximating the performance of non-voice-based systems, with a few areas still needing improvement. Particularly, notable differences emerged in areas such as mental effort, complexity, accuracy, and reusability. These differences were especially pronounced when comparing teleportation to the spherical system, while the gap between teleportation and the Cartesian system was significantly smaller. However, in key aspects critical to VR locomotion, such as motion sickness and presence scores, there were only minor to negligible differences between the interaction modalities. One major difference observed during our study was that the teleportation technique struggled to overcome the lower and upper barricades in our maze. Since teleportation requires a clear line of sight to the destination, blocked vision made it considerably more cumbersome to reach the target location. For the lower barricade, users typically teleported close to the wall and stretched their arms upward to point to the other side. In contrast, for the upper barricade, users often crouched down in an attempt to aim beyond the obstacle. Voice-based systems, on the other hand, allowed participants to simply articulate the number of the target location on the other side of the wall, enabling them to overcome such obstacles effortlessly and without the need for additional physical adjustments. This difference likely explains why the teleportation technique required more commands to complete the maze task compared to the voice-based systems.}

\reftext{With this in mind, we can generally conclude that while voice-based systems can still be further refined to more closely match approaches like teleportation, they already offer unique opportunities. Especially in scenarios where controllers are infeasible and techniques like teleportation are not available, voice-based locomotion, particularly using the Cartesian system, emerges as a viable alternative. Although it does not yet perfectly match the performance of the teleportation approach, it demonstrates strong potential to closely approximate the baseline.}

\subsection{RQ4: Interaction patterns}
\reftext{To explore focus areas during hands-free locomotion, we closely examined the interaction patterns associated with each task and system. The maze setup was intentionally designed to limit participants' field of view, featuring narrow corridors that restricted visibility. In contrast, the platform environment offered wide areas with free movement. Despite these differences, both the restricted and unrestricted configuration showed a clear pattern: participants primarily interacted with areas near their central view direction, as illustrated in Figs. 8, 10, 9 and 11. While keeping the navigation within the field of view was expected to some degree, we observed a consistent alignment of the navigation direction with the user's central view across all techniques. This pattern was not limited to the voice-based approaches. It was also evident in the baseline teleportation technique, where users rarely deviated from their forward gaze, despite having the flexibility to do so easily. Therefore, we believe this behavior across all techniques is driven more by user preference rather than by constraints imposed by the locomotion design. A slight bias towards the center may be attributed to the design of the cylindrical and spherical approaches, both of which define 0 degrees directly at the center of the user's field of view. In contrast, the Cartesian approach displays all values simultaneously, enabling users to more easily deviate from the central rows. In further contrast, the teleportation system lacks a defined central reference entirely, providing users with complete freedom to select any destination within the environment.}

\reftext{These findings suggest a strong alignment between visual attention and interaction zones, even when participants were given greater freedom of movement. We therefore recommend focusing primarily on the central field of view when developing locomotion techniques to avoid cluttering the virtual space with unnecessary information. The specific design and implementation of these techniques remain an area for future research.}

\subsection{RQ5: Voice locomotion}
\reftext{To address the overarching question of whether voice commands can support locomotion in areas where landmarks are absent, we have considered the discussion of Research Questions [Rq1] - [Rq4], as well as objective and subjective measures and user feedback. Based on this analysis, we can confidently assert that voice commands can serve as a viable alternative for navigation in scenarios where the use of physical controllers is impractical or infeasible. While non-voicebased approaches demonstrate higher accuracy, faster time-to-target, and better overall usability, voice-based systems still receive positive evaluations across all questionnaire metrics and can compete with the performance of non-voice-based systems. The most significant difference lies in the time-to-target, where the teleportation approach remains substantially faster. However, the voice-based approaches enhance system accessibility, allowing individuals who might otherwise struggle to interact with the environment to do so more effectively. It also supports both self-locomotion and object manipulation in environments where physical controllers are infeasible or impractical. An interesting direction for future work would be to investigate whether the post-study ratings correspond with physiological measurements such as eye tracking, heart rate, or skin conductance. Such data could further provide objective insights into user engagement, cognitive load, and emotional responses during interaction. While the Cartesian, cylindrical, and spherical systems performed similarly in terms of overall performance and accuracy, we observed a clear advantage in time to target and user satisfaction with the Cartesian system. We attribute this improvement to its simpler position encoding. Consequently, we recommend adopting a more straightforward encoding method, as it significantly enhances usability. The insights into interaction patterns can further guide the development of specialized locomotion systems, enabling more intuitive and efficient navigation in virtual environments. By aligning locomotion systems with user behavior and preferences, we can enhance the overall effectiveness and user experience in hands-free interaction.}

\subsection{Limitations}
\reftext{While the presented coordinate systems were perceived positively, our approach does pose some inherent limitations. For example, the coordinate systems impose different cognitive loads. The Cartesian system enables users to specify a desired location with a single number, the cylindrical system requires two numbers, whereas the spherical approach often necessitates specifying two angles and a distance, resulting in a higher cognitive demand. Although our intent was to investigate these cognitive differences, we acknowledge the inherent limitations imposed by cognitive disparities. Furthermore, our approach focused exclusively on user-centered coordinate systems. While the presented systems could also be applied as world-space coordinate systems, their performance in this context will need to be evaluated in future work. An additional limitation of our approach may be the range of visible numbers. While in our study even the most distant numbers remained clearly visible to participants, scaling this approach to larger environments or greater distances may present challenges. In such cases, the visibility of the numbers displayed on the ground would need to be carefully considered and potentially adapted to ensure continued usability. Furthermore, each participant spent approximately 15 min using each technique, which is relatively brief for evaluating long-term usability. Once a suitable technique is identified, further investigation should be conducted to assess how users perform with these techniques during more extended usage sessions. Additionally, the study was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions. The integration of voice-based systems into everyday workflows is beyond the scope of this work and should be explored in future research. This also applies to the intended target group. In our study, most participants came from fields such as information technology or mathematics. As a result, working with coordinate systems may have been easier for them compared to the general population. An in-depth analysis involving diverse target groups is planned for future research.}

\reftext{Traditional input methods, such as physical controllers, may not always be optimal and can, in some cases, even be impractical [3]. In such cases, voice-controlled approaches can offer an effective alternative for interacting with the environment. However, articulating a desired locomotion destination can be challenging in spaces without discernible landmarks, such as empty rooms, or when multiple instances of the same object are present, as in a forest filled with numerous trees.}

\reftext{We address this challenge by facilitating hands-free locomotion and interaction in landmark-free environments through the development of a system that visually aids in selecting a location. We therefore compare Cartesian, cylindrical, and spherical coordinate systems approaches to the non-voice-based approach of teleportation. We evaluate all systems using both objective and subjective measures to assess performance, accuracy, and user satisfaction. We found that while all voice-based systems performed similarly in accuracy, the Cartesian and cylindrical systems resulted in faster task completion and higher user satisfaction due to their simpler position encoding. While our non-voice-based baseline demonstrated slightly higher performance in most categories, the voice-based systems, particularly the Cartesian approach, came very close in terms of both performance and user satisfaction. Consequently, in environments where controllers are unavailable or impractical, voice-based systems offer a viable and effective alternative for interacting with digital environments, even in the absence of landmarks.}

\reftext{Furthermore, we observed that participants consistently interacted with areas close to their central view direction, regardless of the system used or the degree of freedom provided. This interaction pattern highlights the importance of designing systems that align with natural user behavior, which is crucial for developing more intuitive and accessible interactive systems in virtual reality and other digital environments. While the performance of the different systems is relatively similar, personal preferences and subjective evaluations can vary across techniques. This underscores the importance of usability and intuitiveness, which are critical factors for the successful adoption of new interaction methods.}

\reftext{Our work advances the broader field of virtual reality by enhancing accessibility and user experience, moving us closer to more intuitive and inclusive interactive systems. It specifically advances the field of voice-controlled interaction by offering a solution to the challenge of articulating position commands in environments lacking discernible landmarks.}
